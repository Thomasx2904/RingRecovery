Which ring metals a detector picks up

A metal detector responds to metal that conducts electricity. How well your ring conducts, how big it is, how deep it has gone and the ground around it all shape the signal it gives. Here’s how common ring materials compare.

  • Gold (yellow, white or rose). Yes. But a ring is a small, thin target, and gold can read in the same range as foil and ring-pulls. That’s why I check every signal in the search area instead of skipping the ones that sound like rubbish.
  • Silver. Yes. Silver conducts electricity better than any other metal, so it tends to give a clear signal.
  • Platinum. Yes. Platinum conducts far less well than silver, so a platinum ring can read low, in the same range as many gold rings.
  • Titanium. Yes. It’s a poor conductor, so a titanium ring can give a weaker signal than a gold ring of the same size. A slow, careful search matters.
  • Tungsten carbide. Yes. Detectorists do recover tungsten carbide rings. How one reads varies from ring to ring, so I check every signal.
  • Ceramic, silicone and wood. No. These aren’t metals, and ceramics generally don’t conduct electricity, so a metal detector can’t find them. The exception is a ring with a metal core or inlay, so tell me if yours has one.
  • A loose diamond or stone. No. If a stone has come out of its setting with no metal attached, a detector can’t find it. If the whole ring is missing, the metal band is what I search for.

Real rings are alloys. An 18-carat gold alloy conducts only a fraction as well as pure gold, so a ring’s size and thickness matter as much as its metal, and so do depth and ground conditions. Treat this as a rough guide, not a promise.

Not sure what yours is made of? Look for a stamp inside the band, check the receipt or valuation, or tell me what you know about it.

When rings slip off

Think back to the moment it might have come off. These are the ones I’d ask about.

  • Cold water. Cold makes the blood vessels in your fingers tighten, so fingers get slimmer and rings get looser. A winter swim, a cold pool or icy hands on a frosty morning can all do it.
  • Sunscreen, lotion and washing up. Slippery hands make it easier for a ring to slide over the knuckle.
  • Throwing and catching. Beach cricket, fetch with the dog, kicking the footy at the park.
  • Gloves, wetsuits and tight sleeves. Pulling something snug off your hand can take a ring with it.
  • Celebrations. Dancing on a lawn, a wedding in a garden, a party in the backyard.

Wherever it was, the first steps are the same. Stop. Don’t dig. Drop a map pin, note the time and get in touch. There’s more detail if you lost it at the beach or on a lawn.

What to tell me

  • What the ring is made of, and roughly how wide and heavy it is.
  • Where you were, when, and what you were doing.
  • Whether anyone has dug, raked, mown or swept the area since.
  • Your map pin and photos, if you have them.
  • If it was at the beach, the time it happened, because the tide matters.

Questions people ask

Wedding rings, answered.

Can you search where we got married?

If it’s a venue or a private garden, we’ll need the owner’s okay before I search. Tell me where it was and roughly where you were standing.

The diamond fell out. Can you find it?

Not with a metal detector, sorry. A stone on its own isn’t metal. If the band is lost too, that’s what I’d search for.

It’s been a while. Is it worth trying?

Sometimes. It depends on where it was and what’s happened there since. Tell me the story and I’ll give you a straight answer.

Will it cost more because my ring is valuable?

No. My price is for my time, never for the sentimental or dollar value of what you lost.
