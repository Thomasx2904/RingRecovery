Why the first 24 hours matter

A lost ring doesn't stay where you dropped it.

01

The sand moves

Every tide reworks the top layer of a beach. Something dropped on soft wet sand in the afternoon can sit well below the surface by morning, and deeper means slower and harder to pin down.

02

You're not the only one detecting

The popular metropolitan beaches get swept by hobbyists regularly, especially after a hot weekend. If someone else picks it up first, there is nothing left to find.

03

The memory of the spot fades

Every search starts with where you were standing. That memory gets vague within a day or two, and a tight search area is the single biggest factor in whether an item turns up.

How a recovery runs

Four steps, start to finish.

No apps, no deposits, no waiting on a booking system. You call, and we work out together whether a search is worth doing.

01

Call or message me

Tell me what you lost and roughly where. I'll ask about the ground and the timing, then tell you honestly whether I think it's findable.

02

We agree a time

Often the same day. For a beach search I work around low tide, which usually means an early start or an evening — whatever gives the best shot at the ground.

03

I grid and sweep the area

I mark out the search zone and work it in overlapping lines so no ground is missed, then pinpoint and dig carefully. You're welcome to be there, and it genuinely helps.

04

You get it straight back

If I find it, it goes back in your hand on the spot. The callout covers my time either way, so there is nothing further to settle.

What turns up

Anything metal, and most of it is jewellery.

Wedding and engagement rings are the bulk of the work, but a detector doesn't care what the item is — only what it's made of.

  • Wedding rings
  • Engagement rings
  • Bracelets
  • Necklaces
  • Earrings
  • Watches
  • Phones
  • Car & house keys
  • Coins & keepsakes

Solid gold, silver, platinum, titanium and steel all read well. An all-ceramic or silicone ring, or a loose stone out of its setting, won't register — a detector only finds metal.

Questions people ask first

Straight answers.

How soon can you get there?

Often the same day. Ring me and I'll tell you what's realistic — for beach work the tide matters as much as my diary, and sometimes waiting six hours for low water is the better call.

What happens if you don't find it?

The callout still applies, because the time and the travel are the same either way. What I will always do is give you my honest read on the odds before you commit, and if I don't think it's findable I'll tell you that on the phone rather than take the job.

Can you search in water?

Shallow water, yes — the swash zone and knee to waist depth, depending on conditions and how rough it is. Tell me roughly how deep you were and I'll tell you straight whether it's within reach.

How deep can a detector actually find a ring?

It depends on the item, the ground and the conditions. A small gold ring in beach sand is usually within reach in the top layer of sand; larger items read deeper. Wet salt sand and mineralised ground are harder going than dry sand or grass.

Do I need to be there while you search?

It helps enormously, because you can stand where you were standing and narrow the area down. If you can't make it, a clear description or a dropped pin on a map works — just be as precise as you can.

Do you need permission to detect?

Most public beaches and reserves are fine. Some council reserves, sports grounds and all private property need the owner's or the council's say-so first — I'll sort that out with you before we search rather than turn up and get moved on.

Will you dig up my lawn?

No. On grass I cut a small plug, lift it, retrieve the item and put it back. Done properly you won't see where I've been within a day or two.

Do you keep the item until I pay?

Never. If I find it, you get it back on the spot. The fee is for my time and travel, not a ransom on your ring.
